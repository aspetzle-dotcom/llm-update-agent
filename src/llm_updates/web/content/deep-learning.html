The artificial neuron

A neuron takes some numbers in, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up with a bias, and passes the total through an activation function:

output = activation(w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + … + b)

Without the activation, that is just linear regression. The activation is what lets networks bend and represent curved, complicated patterns.

Activation functions

  • ReLU (max(0, x)): the workhorse. Cheap, and its slope never shrinks for positive inputs.
  • Sigmoid squashes to 0…1, handy for a final probability. Its slope is nearly zero at the ends, which slows learning in deep stacks (the vanishing gradient problem).
  • Tanh is like sigmoid but centred on zero, from −1 to 1.
  • GELU is a smooth ReLU used in most transformers, including LLMs.

Turn on “show slope”: training depends on these slopes, so a flat region means a neuron learns slowly.

Layers and networks

Neurons are arranged in layers. The input layer holds your data, hidden layers transform it, and the output layer gives the answer. “Deep” just means many hidden layers. Each layer builds on the one before: in an image model, early layers detect edges, middle layers detect textures and parts, and late layers detect whole objects.

How training works

  1. Forward pass: run a batch of examples through the network to get predictions.
  2. Loss: compare predictions with the right answers. Classification usually uses cross-entropy; regression uses mean squared error.
  3. Backpropagation: use the chain rule of calculus to work out, for every weight, which direction would reduce the loss (its gradient).
  4. Update: move every weight a small step in that direction. The step size is the learning rate.

One pass over the whole dataset is an epoch. Training usually takes many epochs, using small random mini-batches at each step (stochastic gradient descent).

Gradient descent

Picture the loss as a landscape and the weights as your position. Gradient descent always steps downhill. The learning rate decides how big each step is.

Things to try: on the stretched bowl, a learning rate around 0.18 zig-zags and anything above 0.2 explodes. Momentum carries speed between steps, smoothing the zig-zag. On “two valleys”, a start on the right can get stuck in the shallower valley, a local minimum. Optimizers like Adam adapt the step size for each weight automatically, and they are the default for most deep learning.

Convolutions: how networks see

A convolutional neural network (CNN) slides small filters across an image. Each filter is a 3×3 grid of weights; at every position it multiplies the pixels under it and adds them up, producing a feature map that lights up wherever its pattern appears.

Here the filters are hand-picked. In a real CNN the network learns thousands of filter weights during training, and stacks of them recognise faces, cars or tumours.

In practice

  • Data beats cleverness. More, cleaner, more varied data usually helps more than a fancier model.
  • Watch both curves. Plot training and validation loss. If validation loss rises while training loss falls, you are overfitting.
  • Regularize. Dropout (randomly switching neurons off during training), weight decay and early stopping all reduce overfitting.
  • Normalize. Batch norm and layer norm keep values in a healthy range so deep stacks train reliably.
  • Transfer learning. Start from a model pretrained on a huge dataset and fine-tune it on yours. That is far cheaper than training from scratch.
  • Hardware. Training is mostly big matrix multiplications, which is why GPUs and TPUs matter.

Popular libraries: PyTorch (most research and LLM work), JAX and TensorFlow/Keras.
