Classical machine learning

Before neural networks, there was, and still is, a toolbox of simpler models that learn patterns from tables of data. They are fast, explainable, and often all you need.

What “learning” means

A machine learning model is a function with adjustable settings, called parameters. You show it examples, it measures how wrong its answers are with a loss, and an algorithm nudges the parameters to make the loss smaller. Once trained, the model should give good answers on examples it has never seen.

  • Supervised learning: every example has the right answer attached (a label). Predict house prices, flag spam.
  • Unsupervised learning: no labels. Find structure, such as groups of similar customers.
  • Reinforcement learning: an agent acts, gets rewards, and learns which actions pay off.

The single most important habit: keep some data aside as a test set that the model never trains on. That is the only honest way to know how it will do in the real world.

One-hot encoding

A model cannot multiply “red” by a number, and coding red=1, green=2, blue=3 would wrongly suggest blue is “more” than red. One-hot encoding gives each category its own column instead:

colouris_redis_greenis_blue
red100
blue001
green010

Missing values need a decision too: drop the row, fill in the average (imputation), or add an “is missing” column so the model can learn whether missingness itself means something.

Preparing features: scaling

House size runs into thousands of square feet; bedrooms run from 1 to 6. Models that measure distance (k-nearest neighbours, k-means) or use gradient descent would treat size as hundreds of times more important, just because its numbers are bigger. Scaling puts features on comparable ranges.

  • Min–max maps each feature to 0…1: (x − min) / (max − min).
  • Standardization gives mean 0 and standard deviation 1: (x − mean) / std. It is the usual default.

Compute the min, max, mean and standard deviation from the training set only, then apply those same numbers to the test set. Otherwise information from the test set leaks into training.

Regression: predicting a number

Linear regression fits a straight line y = a·x + b through the data. “Best” means the line with the smallest mean squared error: the average of the squared vertical gaps between each point and the line. Squaring makes big misses count much more than small ones.

Notice how a single outlier drags the whole line. R² tells you what share of the variation in y the line explains: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than always guessing the average. With many features the idea is the same, just a plane or hyperplane instead of a line.

Classification: predicting a category

k-nearest neighbours is the simplest classifier there is: to label a new point, look at the k closest training points and take a vote. There is no training step at all; the data is the model.

With k = 1 the boundary wraps tightly around every single point, including noisy ones. Larger k gives smoother, more general regions. Leave-one-out accuracy classifies each point using all the others, a quick stand-in for test accuracy.

Other classic classifiers: logistic regression (a straight boundary with a probability attached), decision trees (a flowchart of yes/no questions), random forests and gradient-boosted trees (many trees voting; often the strongest choice for tables), and support vector machines (the boundary with the widest margin).

Clustering: finding groups without labels

k-means repeats two steps: assign every point to its nearest centre, then move each centre to the average of its points. It stops when nothing changes.

Try the wrong k and different starting centres: k-means always finds some answer, but it can split a real group in two or merge two groups. Inertia measures how tight the clusters are; it always drops as k grows, so look for the “elbow” where adding clusters stops helping much.

Overfitting and underfitting

A model that is too simple misses the pattern (underfitting). A model that is too flexible memorises the noise in its training data and fails on new data (overfitting). This is the bias–variance trade-off.

Training error keeps falling as the degree goes up. Test error falls, then rises again. The bottom of the test curve is the sweet spot. Ways to fight overfitting: more data, simpler models, regularization (penalising large parameters, as in ridge and lasso regression), and cross-validation to choose settings.

Measuring a classifier

Most classifiers output a score, and you pick a threshold above which you say “positive”. Moving it trades one kind of mistake for the other.

  • Accuracy: share of all answers that are right. Misleading when one class is rare.
  • Precision: of everything flagged positive, how much really was. Matters when false alarms are costly.
  • Recall: of all real positives, how many were caught. Matters when misses are costly, such as in disease screening.
  • F1: a single number balancing precision and recall.

For regression, use mean squared error, its square root (RMSE, in the target's own units), or mean absolute error.
